A Creative Horse Project for School and Home

Looking for an animal shelter model or something practical to make for holiday homework? The stable, scenery and paper animals are already illustrated, so you can spend your time cutting, folding and building instead of drawing every piece from scratch.

For an EVS assignment in Class 3, 4 or 5, use the display to introduce domestic animals, the shelter people provide and everyday animal care. Ask your teacher whether a printable model suits the assignment. This is a creative paper scene, not a model of a particular Indian farm or a prescribed curriculum resource.

At home, share the tasks: an adult can help with smaller cuts while children arrange the horses, choose where the fences go and tell a story about their stable. It also works as a homeschool craft or a screen-free weekend activity.

Print, Cut and Build Your Horse Diorama

1

Print

Print the PDF and identify the backgrounds, stable, horses and accessories. Regular printer paper works well.

2

Cut and Fold

Cut around the pieces and keep their glue tabs. Fold the tabs before applying glue; adults can help with small details.

3

Build and Arrange

Fit the landscape inside your shoebox, assemble the stable and arrange the fences and horses to give the scene depth.

Start with the Shoebox Landscape

The upright box walls form the countryside behind the stable, while the base becomes a grassy paddock. Try the background pieces in place before gluing. Trim or overlap them to fit the box you have, keeping the landscape aligned across the corners.

No special shoebox size is required. A larger box gives you more room to arrange the horses and accessories. Let the background glue dry before adding the standing pieces.

Build the Stable and Add the Small Details

Layered paper pieces bring the stable forward from the backdrop. The open doors reveal the illustrated interior; fences and flowers frame the paddock without hiding the horses.

Turn the Model into an EVS Presentation

After building, children can add their own labels and practise a short explanation in their own words. Start with these questions:

  • What is the difference between a sheltered stable and an outdoor paddock?
  • Which parts of your scene represent shelter, water and space for the horses?
  • What care would a real horse need each day?
  • How did you turn flat printed sheets into a 3D model?

These are extra learning ideas for your school presentation, not additional worksheets in the PDF. You could make your own labels for “stable”, “horse”, “fence” and “water”, then describe the model to a family member before taking it to class.
